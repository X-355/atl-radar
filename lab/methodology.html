How the Research Lab measures bottoms, ATL retests, futures signals, the paper bots and the Polymarket study
Research Lab ATL Radar

What the Research Lab is

A study of what happens around crypto price bottoms: what big orders do before a low, how longs with their liquidation below the all-time low (ATL) would have done, whether futures data could have spotted bottoms in advance, and a paper bot that tracks the one rule that passed our stress test. Sections 5 and 6 are a separate study of Polymarket's 5-minute Bitcoin markets and a live paper test of the one lead it found. The results are worked out offline and published as data files that the page reads. The page itself collects nothing about visitors.

"Sections 1–3 updated" in the header is the date those sections were last calculated; they are recalculated on the 2nd of each month. Section 4, the paper bot, updates every Monday and shows its own update time. Section 5, the Polymarket study, is a one-off study with its dates in its own text. Section 6, Polybot, updates live every 30 minutes. This is research, not financial advice.

Coins and data

  • Spot and ATL sections: coins from today's top 30 on Binance, plus ICP and BSV. The exact count is shown at the top of the Lab page. BSV has no Binance spot market, so its numbers come from Binance futures, where its data starts in October 2023.
  • Futures section: its own list of coins with Binance perpetual futures, from January 2022 onward.
  • Source: Binance's public data archive. Daily candles for prices; every executed trade (aggTrades) for the days around each event, with fills from the same taker order in the same millisecond merged into one order. Order sizes are in US dollars at the trade price.
  • Big orders are taker (market) orders of $100,000 or more. Resting limit orders do not show up in trades; the order-book measures in the futures section are the test for those.

1 · Big orders around bottoms

  • A bottom is the lowest price within 14 days either side, at least 30% below the prior 90-day high, and followed by a 25% rally.
  • Each bottom is compared with a falling knife on the same coin (also down 30%, but the price kept falling at least another 10%) and with a random day.
  • Every measure is relative to that coin's normal level, so 0 means a typical day for that coin. Volume is in % above normal, net buying in % of all volume, and buying shares in percentage points.
  • p vs knives comes from a Mann-Whitney test, which compares the two groups by rank rather than by average. It is the chance of seeing a gap this large between bottoms and falling knives if there were really no difference. Small values (below about 0.05) mean the gap is unlikely to be luck. The p in the "Did big orders show up at retests that held?" table is the same test, comparing retests that held with ones that broke.
  • Separation is the chance that a real bottom scores higher than a falling knife on that measure: 0.5 is a coin flip, and below 0.5 means bottoms score lower.
  • The days after the low are partly built in, because a bottom is defined by the rally that follows. Part of the selling before the low is also mechanical, since the last days before a low are usually down days; the falling-knife comparison controls for some, not all, of that.

2 · Longs with liquidation below the ATL

  • The ATL is the lowest price since the coin listed on Binance, not its all-time low everywhere.
  • A retest is a day when the price came back within 10% of the ATL, where that ATL was set at least 30 days earlier. Outcomes are measured over the next 30 days: held means the price never traded below the ATL in that time.
  • How the trade would have done: a long opened at the retest day's daily close and closed at the daily close 30 days later. Costs are a 0.05% fee each way plus funding at a typical 0.01% every 8 hours (about 0.9% over 30 days), not the funding actually charged at the time. Each setup either places the liquidation a set distance below the ATL (which fixes the leverage, capped at 125×) or uses a fixed leverage, with a 1% maintenance margin.
  • A position is liquidated if any daily low in the 30 days reaches its liquidation price, and then counts as −100% of the margin. Because only daily lows are checked, a wick inside a day counts, but the order of moves within a day does not.
  • Averages are pulled up by a few big winners, so the median and the share profitable show a typical trade better.

ATL Radar also counts retests, but from weekly candles with an 8-week window on the coins it shows, so its numbers differ from these.

3 · Futures signals

  • A possible-bottom day is a day when a coin's perpetual future is at least 25% below its 30-day high, judged at 00:00 UTC using only past data.
  • A trade buys at the next hour's open and holds 72 hours, with an 8% stop-loss, a 0.05% fee each way and funding paid, at 1× (no leverage). The exact settings are also shown on the Lab page.
  • Funding here is Binance's actual historical funding rate for each coin, from its public archive (not an assumed rate as in the ATL trades). The rates at the 00:00, 08:00 and 16:00 UTC settlements during the 72 hours are added up: a long pays when the rate is positive and receives when it is negative. Two simplifications: the full 72 hours of funding is counted even when the stop-loss closes a trade early, and coins that settle every 4 hours are only charged at those three times a day.
  • Each signal was defined on 2022–2023 data and then tested, unchanged, on 2024 onward, which it had never seen. A signal "holds up" only if it beats buying on a random day by more than luck would explain in the test period.
  • t measures how sure we can be that an average isn't luck; below about 2 (either sign) it could easily be chance. It is corrected for trades that overlap in time.
  • Liquidated at 5× is the share of trades that would have been liquidated with 5× leverage.
  • The bar chart shows the rank correlation between each measure on a possible-bottom day and the return over the next 72 hours: 0 means no link, and ±0.1 would already be useful.
  • Binance's order-book depth archive starts in 2023, so the order-book signal has only a handful of 2022–2023 trades.

4 · Paper bot: the Bitcoin 20-week guard

  • Rule: after each weekly candle closes (Monday 00:00 UTC), hold BTC if the weekly close is above the average of the last 20 weekly closes, otherwise hold USDT. One decision a week, no leverage, no shorts, no stop-loss.
  • Paper account: starts at 1,000 USDT; when holding, it moves with BTC's weekly close; each switch between BTC and USDT costs 0.1%. "Just holding BTC" starts from the same 1,000 USDT on the same week. Data: Binance's public BTCUSDT weekly candles. The bot has no exchange keys and places no orders.
  • Why this rule: a stress test re-built the top 10, 20 and 50 Binance USDT coins week by week from 2018, including coins that were later delisted, and tried 4, 8, 12 and 26-week momentum and the 20-week average, at 0.1%, 0.2% and 0.4% fees. On altcoin baskets every version failed from 2024 on once dead coins were included. On Bitcoin alone the 20-week average held up from every start year and at every fee level, mainly by cutting the worst drop roughly in half.
  • Limits: the 20-week average is a well-known rule, and 2018–2026 holds only about 20 in-and-out cycles, a small sample. It lags: it steps out after a fall has started and back in after a rise has started, and in choppy markets it can switch at a loss. In the stress test, weeks with missing candles counted as no change.

5 · Polymarket 5-minute Bitcoin markets

  • Data: a public recording of Polymarket's live order-book and price feeds for its 5-minute "Up or Down" markets (Hugging Face dataset polydata5/polymarket-updown-5m-ws, CC-BY-4.0). We used Bitcoin days where at least 1,380 of the 1,440 minutes were captured for both the order book and the price feed: 73 days from 10 September to 25 November 2026, 20,995 markets.
  • Order books: rebuilt from the recorded snapshots and every price-level change. Checked on 126 million updates, our rebuilt best bid and ask matched the exchange's own figures 99.86% of the time. Each market was read at 4, 3, 2 and 1 minute, 30 seconds and 10 seconds before its end, and again 1 and 2 seconds later. Quotes older than 30 seconds were skipped.
  • Results: Polymarket's official outcome of each market, read from its public market data. "Up" wins if the Chainlink BTC/USD 60-second average price (TWAP) at the end of the window is at least the 60-second average at its start (the "price to beat"). Our own reading of the recorded price feed gave the same result for 100% of markets.
  • Price-vs-start model: a formula with nothing fitted. It estimates the final 60-second average from the latest Chainlink price and the part of the final minute already seen, takes the volatility from the previous 5 minutes, and turns the gap to the price to beat into a probability.
  • Gradient tree model: a gradient-boosted tree classifier (the kind of model in the "Quant Tree" posts) using the price vs the start price, the formula's estimate and its uncertainty, volatility, seconds left, the "Up" price, both spreads, the size waiting at each best ask, and how far the two asks add up past $1. It was trained on 60% of the days and scored on the other 40%, then the whole test was repeated with the halves swapped.
  • Trades: buy one side at the best ask and hold it to the result: a winning share pays $1, a losing one pays nothing. At most one trade per market per moment. A model trades when its probability minus the ask and the fee is more than 2¢ (or 5¢). Fee: Polymarket's published taker fee for these markets, 0.05 × price × (1 − price) per share (1.25¢ at 50¢); results were also run with no fee and with 0.072. "Return" is profit per $1 spent.
  • Forecast accuracy: the Brier score, the average squared gap between the forecast probability and what happened (lower is better), compared with the market's own mid-price on the same moments.
  • Limits: the backtest assumes our order fills at the recorded best ask. In real trading, faster bots can take that price first, and the recording's timestamps are when the recorder received each message, so a live bot would usually be slower still. The median size at the best ask was 353 shares, enough for small trades only. 73 days is a short stretch of market conditions. About 36 rule-and-moment combinations were tested, so a few can look good by chance; the last-30-seconds result counts as a lead because it held on both halves, not as proof. Polymarket has changed its fees several times in 2026. This is a one-off study, dated above; it is not updated automatically.

6 · Polybot: live paper test

  • What it is: a paper-trading bot (no wallet, no keys, no orders, no real money) running 24/7 on a small cloud server (Amazon Lightsail, Mumbai). Code and full logs are public at github.com/X-355/polybot.
  • Inputs: Polymarket's public order book for the current 5-minute Bitcoin market and the Chainlink BTC/USD price feed the market settles on, both live over WebSockets.
  • Decisions: 30 and 10 seconds before each window closes, it computes the same features as the section 5 backtest and runs the same gradient-tree model, trained on all 73 days of section 5. It buys one side at the best ask with a pretend $10 only if the model's probability minus the ask and the taker fee is more than 2¢. Taker fee: 0.05 × price × (1 − price) per share, Polymarket's published rate for these markets.
  • Freshness check: the bot compares Polymarket's timestamp on each order-book message with when it processed it, and skips the decision if its view is more than 2 seconds behind, or if the price feed is more than 10 seconds old. The first 6 trades (27 November 2026) were made before this check existed. They are shown but excluded, because during a fast market the bot was seeing prices that no longer existed.
  • Scoring: each trade is scored against Polymarket's official result at the worse of the price when the bot decided and the price one second later, after the fee. "Return per $1" is profit divided by money spent. The fee is paid on top of the stake, so a trade that loses everything comes out slightly worse than −100%. The "always buy the favourite" figure uses the same windows and moments as the bot's trades, so it is just as noisy until many trades are in.
  • The rules, fixed in advance (27 November 2026): no verdict before 300 counted trades. Edge confirmed only if the 95% interval of return per $1 is above zero and the bot beats always buying the favourite in the same windows at the same moments. The two decisions in one window are treated as related when computing the interval. Interval below zero: no edge. Still unclear at 1,000 trades: not proven, and the test ends.
  • Limits: a paper trade assumes our order would have filled at the best ask. In about half the early trades, less than $10 was on offer at that price, and a real order also competes with faster bots. So even a passing result would be an upper bound on what a real bot could earn.

Limits to keep in mind

  • Survivorship: every coin studied is still trading today, mostly coins that recovered. Coins that collapsed and left the rankings are missing, which makes bottoms and ATL retests look better than they were.
  • Sample size: one coin has only a few dozen bottoms and a handful of retests, so per-coin numbers swing a lot. Use them to see whether a coin follows the overall pattern, not as a signal.
  • Binance only: other exchanges can show different lows and different order flow.
  • Past patterns can stop working, as several futures signals did after 2023.
  • No real trades: sections 4 and 6 are paper bots and section 5 replays recorded data. Polymarket is not available in every country, so check your local rules before using it. This is research, not financial advice.
